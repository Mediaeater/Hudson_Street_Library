A Library of Libraries

Institutions endow holdings. Nobody has yet endowed judgment. A library of libraries, each collection kept whole — open to its first patrons.

Hudson Street Library July 2026 8 min read

TL;DR / The Ask

This is a proposal with a cost, and the window on it is closing.

A collection of collections is the only acquisition left that can't be outbid, front-run, or bought at auction next spring. It has to be built.

The great private collections of the last fifty years are being broken up right now, not eventually. The trade has its own shorthand for why collections come to market: death, debt, divorce. The three D's are the actual acquisition calendar, and they are indifferent to whether anyone is ready. The generation that assembled these libraries, book by book, at real personal expense, is downsizing and dying, and the default outcome is the lot sale: a life's judgment sorted by subject heading, priced by the shelf-foot, and dissolved. The books mostly survive somewhere. What disappears is the argument they were arranged to make, and that is unrecoverable. Nobody rebuilds a collection. They only ever build a different one.

At the same time institutions are converting from ownership to license. So happy I lived through owning my own media, anyway. Their holdings are contractual, revocable, logged, and subject to whoever renews the vendor agreement.

Civilization is not the accumulation of objects but the maintenance of judgment across generations, and judgment is transmitted by curation, not coverage. A library of curated libraries, each kept whole and attributed, is the only structure that preserves the judgment rather than just the inventory.

It requires two things that concept does not come with: a building and a budget. Yeah, this could be a blocker.

A building, because the unit of the collection is the collection, and collections kept whole require shelf and climate and floor. A budget, because acquisition, transport, cataloguing at the level of provenance rather than subject, and permanent stewardship are staff costs, not enthusiasm costs. The number is knowable: $8 million to open in contributed space, $2 million a year to run, $45 million to endow. $100 million buys the building and founds it outright.

This is addressed to two people.

The first can afford to enable it. Everything that survives from a culture survives because someone with means decided it should, before there was any proof it would matter. (There are maybe forty people in this city for whom the entire operating budget is a rounding error on a single canvas. You know who you are. Several of you already own the building.) That decision has never once been made by a market. It is made by a person who understood the moment they were standing in. This is that moment, and it is short.

The second has spent thirty years building something and knows exactly what it cost. You already know what happens to it by default. The offer is the alternative: your library, kept whole, in your order, under your name, permanently legible as the work it is. Housed as authored, not dissolved into a subject catalog or reduced to a plaque on a wing.

An accession standard, provenance-level cataloguing, the terms under which a collection is placed, and how the federation works across holdings are already written up as separate documents, available on request. This page sticks to the ask.

❖ ❖ ❖

An institutional library collects by mandate. It has an accession policy, a committee, a budget line, and an obligation to coverage. Its ambition is completeness within a defined scope, and its highest virtue is that it does not editorialize. Ask it what matters and it will hand you everything.

A personal library has no mandate. It has a wall, a ceiling, a household, an income. Every book on it displaced a book that isn't. That constraint is the source of the private library's value: scarcity forces judgment, and judgment is information.

A collection assembled under real constraint carries the shape of a mind, and no institution can manufacture that. The choice to keep a slim exhibition catalog from a gallery that closed in 1987 while passing on the definitive monograph of a more famous contemporary is an argument. It's an unpublished essay, written in objects, revisable at any time, never finished. The order on the shelf is part of the text. Adjacency is authorship: when someone shelves Hujar next to a surveillance-camera manual rather than next to other portraitists, they have said something no catalog record can encode.

The corollary matters more. In a personal library, the omission is legible. You can see what a collector refused. Institutional catalogs have no omission signal at all, because absence there means only that a purchase order never cleared. When everything is theoretically in scope, nothing is a decision.

What institutions structurally miss

The material most worth preserving is often the material institutions collect worst. Print runs of three hundred. Zines. Self-published photobooks. Artist editions with no ISBN, no distributor, no review, and no MARC record. Ephemera from spaces that existed for eighteen months. The acquisition machinery of a large library is calibrated to trade publishing, which means it works well precisely where the market has already done the preservation work and fails where nobody else is looking.

Private collectors get there first because they are not buying for a category. They are buying because they were in the room.

There is a second problem, newer and worse. The institutional collection is increasingly licensed rather than owned: vendor platforms, per-seat digital access, patron activity logged by a third party, titles that can be revoked from a catalog by a contract renegotiation or removed by political pressure. A library that cannot survive the loss of a vendor relationship is not a library, it is a subscription. Meanwhile the private shelf remains unmetered and unlogged, the last reading environment with no observer. Nobody knows how many times a book on it has been opened.

Distribution is also defense. A single institutional collection is a single point of removal. Ten thousand private ones are not.

The failure mode of private libraries

Personal libraries die. This is their one structural weakness against the institution and it is severe. The collector dies, and the collection is broken into lots, sold by the shelf-foot, absorbed into other holdings, or given wholesale to an institution that will file it by subject and dissolve the very adjacencies that made it a work. The judgment evaporates. What survives is the inventory, which was never the interesting part.

The question is whether the durability of institutions can be had without their flattening.

The proposal

A library composed of other libraries, each kept whole.

A merged catalog destroys the signal: a union of ten collections is just a bigger pile with the attribution stripped. What's wanted is federation with provenance intact: this shelf, in this order, assembled by this person, for these reasons, with these refusals. The unit of the collection is the collection, not the volume.

Under that model the curated library of curated libraries becomes something an institution genuinely cannot be. It is a set of positions rather than a coverage map. You consult it the way you consult a group of people whose taste you trust, not the way you consult an index. The overlaps tell you what a community of serious attention has converged on. The disjoint holdings tell you where individual conviction went out ahead of consensus. Both are readings you can only get if the boundaries between collections are preserved.

Selection into such a library has to be selective for the same reason the constituent collections are valuable: a federation that accepts everything reproduces the institution it was meant to answer. The curation is applied twice. Once by each collector on their own shelf, once at the door.

And the effect on the collector is not incidental. A shelf that is legible to others is a shelf that has to hold up. Publishing a collection turns it from an accumulation into a claim, and claims get defended, refined, corrected. Institutions produce holdings. This produces argument.

The unit of the collection is the collection, not the volume.

This proposal is a working one, and it is addressed to two kinds of reader. If you keep a library that argues, a shelf with refusals in it, we want to know it exists. If you are in a position to fund durability, as a patron, a foundation, or a grant program, the founding role is open. Write to us: info@hudsonstreetlibrary.com.

Historical Evolution of the Phillpot Diagram

Clive Phillpot's 1982 taxonomy of the artist book — and Kione Kochi's updated version for Half Letter Press — maps the socioeconomic forces that dictate the life of a book-as-art.

Hudson Street Library April 2026 5 min read
Kione Kochi's update of Clive Phillpot's 1982 Artists' Books diagram, published by Half Letter Press. The revision adds a Digital strata reflecting PDF and Print-on-Demand distribution.

The diagram's history is rooted in the effort to institutionalize and define the "Artist Book" as a specific medium during the late 20th century.

Clive Phillpot, while serving as the Director of the Library at the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), developed the original version of this diagram in 1982. His goal was to provide a rigorous taxonomic structure for librarians and curators. At the time, the term "artist book" was being applied broadly to everything from hand-painted one-offs to mass-market paperbacks.

Phillpot's diagrams were polemic tools designed to isolate "Book Art" as a distinct category where the artist uses the book's specific mechanics — sequencing, paging, and portability — as the primary medium.

The Democratic Multiple

The history of this diagram is tied to the 1960s and 70s movement of the "Democratic Multiple." Artists like Ed Ruscha and groups like Fluxus sought to bypass the gallery system by creating cheap, mass-produced books. Phillpot used the diagram to champion these "multiples" over "book objects" (sculptural, unique works). He argued that the true artist book belonged in the "Multiple" or "Large Print Run" strata because its value lay in its ability to be widely distributed, rather than its rarity.

Reshaping the Institution

Phillpot's work at MoMA shifted how the institution collected these works. By categorizing books based on their position between "Art" and "Books," he moved the acquisition of these items from the curated print departments into the museum library. This established the library as a site of active artistic engagement rather than just a repository for research materials.

The Kione Kochi Update

The version you are looking at was updated by Kione Kochi for Half Letter Press to address the collapse of traditional publishing hierarchies. While the 1982 original focused on the tension between the gallery and the bookstore, the modern update reflects the historical shift toward decentralized production. The inclusion of the "Digital" strata acknowledges that the "Democratic Multiple" has moved from the offset press to the PDF and Print-on-Demand (P.O.D.) services, effectively removing the "Gatekeeper" (the Pear side) from the equation entirely.

The first visual attempt to map the socioeconomic forces — dealers, publishers, and distributors — that dictate the life of a book-as-art.

The diagram remains a standard reference in Art History and Library Science curricula. It serves as the primary conceptual bridge between the Fine Art tradition (centered on the unique object) and the Mass Communication tradition (centered on the disseminated idea). It is historically significant for being the first visual attempt to map the socioeconomic forces — dealers, publishers, and distributors — that dictate the life of a book-as-art.
